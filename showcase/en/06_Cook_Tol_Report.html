2. STEELMANNING

2.1. The Steelmanned Reconstruction of the Cook et al. Thesis

To conduct an unassailable audit, we first reconstruct the strongest possible scientific version of John Cook et al.'s argument:

  1. The Core Question is Paradigm Existence, Not Lexical Accounting:
    The fundamental objective of Cook et al. (2013) was not an abstract econometric exercise in textual frequency, but an empirical question of existential societal importance: Does there exist an active, substantive scientific debate in the peer-reviewed literature regarding whether human activity is driving modern global warming? By assembling the largest surveyed corpus in climate history (11,944 abstracts across 21 years), the authors demonstrated that active peer-reviewed rejection of anthropogenic global warming (AGW) is statistically negligible:

    "We ﬁnd that 66.4% of abstracts expressed no position on AGW, 32.6% endorsed AGW, 0.7% rejected AGW and 0.3% were uncertain about the cause of global warming." [P:0003]
    Out of 11,944 papers, only 78 papers (0.7%) rejected AGW.

  2. The Rational Exclusion of the "No Position" Baseline in Mature Sciences:
    In established scientific paradigms, foundational axioms are not re-stated in the abstracts of specialized empirical investigations. As the authors explicitly argue citing Naomi Oreskes:

    "This result is expected in consensus situations where scientists ‘ . . . generally focus their discussions on questions that are still disputed or unanswered rather than on matters about which everyone agrees ’ (Oreskes 2007, p 72)." [P:0007]
    An astrophysicist studying accretion disks does not re-state Newtonian gravitation in an abstract; a biochemist sequencing a kinase inhibitor does not declare that DNA stores genetic code. Similarly, an atmospheric scientist measuring paleoclimate pollen or Arctic ice sheet thickness does not waste abstract word limits re-affirming basic greenhouse radiative forcing. Therefore, defining consensus as:
    $$\text{Consensus} = \frac{\text{Endorse}}{\text{Endorse} + \text{Reject}} = \frac{3,896}{3,896 + 78 + 40} = 97.1\%$$
    is the mathematically appropriate and established standard in consensus bibliometrics.

  3. Independent Empirical Confirmation via Author Self-Ratings:
    Recognizing that abstracts are compressed shorthand, Cook et al. invited the actual corresponding authors to rate their own complete papers using identical criteria:

    "In a second phase of this study, we invited authors to rate their own papers. Compared to abstract ratings, a smaller percentage of self-rated papers expressed no position on AGW (35.5%). Among self-rated papers expressing a position on AGW, 97.2% endorsed the consensus." [P:0003]
    When authors evaluated their own full-length manuscripts, the "no position" proportion fell from 66.4% to 35.5%, and over half (53.8%) of papers whose abstracts expressed no stance were self-rated by their authors as actively endorsing the consensus [P:0006]. This first-party verification demonstrates that abstract silence was indeed shorthand for research operating inside the consensus paradigm.

2.2. The Steelmanned Reconstruction of the Richard Tol Critique

Richard Tol's methodological challenge represents a formidable critique of bibliometric measurement and communication standards:

  1. The Denominator Manipulation Problem:
    Tol points out that headline messaging (including public statements by world leaders claiming that "97% of climate scientists agree that warming is catastrophic") relies on an aggressive mathematical compression of the denominator. In the raw corpus of 11,944 papers:
  2. Endorse AGW (Levels 1–3): $3,896$ papers ($32.6\%$)
  3. No position on AGW (Level 4): $7,930$ papers ($66.4\%$)
  4. Reject AGW (Levels 5–7): 78 papers ($0.7\%$)

  5. Uncertain (Level 4b): 40 papers ($0.3\%$)
    Only $32.6\%$ of the literature explicitly or implicitly endorses AGW. Omitting the $66.4\%$ neutral baseline creates a rhetorical impression of near-unanimous explicit declaration where none exists in the raw corpus.

  6. Semantic Elasticity and Trivial Endorsement:
    Under Cook et al.'s 7-tier scale, Category 3 ("Implicit endorsement") requires only that a paper assumes greenhouse gas emissions cause warming [P:0005]. Virtually all prominent climate contrarians and skeptics acknowledge that CO_2 is a greenhouse gas and causes some warming. Only 64 papers ($0.54\%$ of the total corpus) explicitly quantified human causation as the dominant driver ($>50\%$, Level 1). Conflating this minimal physical consensus with an endorsement of specific policy urgency constitutes semantic scope expansion.

  7. Rater Independence and Volunteer Bias:
    The rating team comprised 12 primary volunteer raters from the advocacy website Skeptical Science [P:0004], [P:0005]. Cook et al. openly disclosed this vulnerability:

    "Two sources of rating bias can be cited: ﬁrst, given that the raters themselves endorsed the scientiﬁc consensus on AGW, they may have been more likely to classify papers as sharing that endorsement." [P:0008]
    In formal content analysis (Krippendorff, Neuendorf), rater alignment with the advocacy hypothesis risks confirmatory drift, even when mitigated by third-party arbitration.

2.3. EMPIRICAL & METHODOLOGICAL SURGERY: THE THREE CRITICAL FRICTION POINTS

Through deterministic cross-examination of the primary text, we identify three genuine methodological friction points that remain unresolved:

Friction Point 1: The Ambiguity of Category 3 and the "Level 1" Deficit

While Cook et al. report 97.1% consensus, the breakdown across endorsement levels reveals a steep pyramid:
* Level 1 (Explicit with quantification $>50\%$): only 64 papers ($0.54\%$)
* Level 2 (Explicit without quantification): 922 papers ($7.72\%$)
* Level 3 (Implicit endorsement): 2,910 papers ($24.36\%$)
The headline consensus rests overwhelmingly ($74.7\%$ of all endorsement papers) on Level 3 ("Implicit endorsement"). Category 3 papers merely assume climate change is occurring as background context for impact or mitigation research (e.g., studying carbon sequestration in soil [P:0005]). Treating implicit assumption as active empirical endorsement of dominant anthropogenic attribution ($>50\%$) remains a vulnerable methodological leap.

Friction Point 2: The Self-Selection Bias in the 14% Author Response Rate

While author self-ratings provide an essential robustness check, the author survey suffered from a low response rate:

"We emailed 8547 authors an invitation to rate their own papers and received 1200 responses (a 14% response rate)." [P:0006], [P:0007]
In survey methodology, a 14% response rate introduces non-ignorable volunteer self-selection bias. Authors who passionately endorse AGW or work in institutional consensus frameworks may be substantially more motivated to respond than industry researchers or neutral geoscientists.

Friction Point 3: The Gateway Belief Overreach in Policy Translation

The authors open and conclude their paper by linking their bibliometric measurement to public policy support:

"The public perception of a scientiﬁc consensus on AGW is a necessary element in public support for climate policy (Ding et al 2011)." [P:0008]
Translating a bibliometric metric into a public relations instrument ("Gateway Belief Model") creates communicative friction: public communicators frequently stripped away Cook et al.'s technical definition ("among abstracts expressing a position") and marketed the 97% figure as "97% of all climate scientists agree on imminent catastrophe," inviting legitimate statistical debunking by Tol that harmed overall scientific credibility.
