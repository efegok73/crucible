2.2. The Steelman Formulation: The Historical Contingency of Racial Hierarchy

To state Frank M. Snowden, Jr.’s thesis in Blacks in Antiquity in its most formidable, rigorous academic structure, the argument must be appreciated as a foundational intervention in the historiography of human difference:

  1. The Historicization and Demystification of Racism:
    Snowden’s primary intellectual achievement was the complete demolition of the modern racialist assumption that biological racism, color antipathy, and white supremacism are innate, trans-historical features of human psychology. By executing an exhaustive audit of ancient Greek, Hellenistic, Roman, and Early Christian literature and art, Snowden proved that for over a millennium in the ancient Mediterranean basin, somatic blackness (melanchroes, niger) was not linked to moral, intellectual, or genetic inferiority. The modern concept of "race" as an immutable biological caste system was alien to the classical world.
  2. The Non-Racial Structure of Mediterranean Servitude:
    Snowden systematically disproved the retrospective projection of American and European plantation chattel slavery onto antiquity. In Greece and Rome, slavery was a universal legal status generated by war, capture, piracy, and economic indebtedness. The enslaved population was overwhelmingly composed of Europeans (Germans, Gauls, Britons, Thracians, Greeks) and Near Easterners (Syrians, Judeans, Phrygians), with sub-Saharan Africans forming a minor, integrated demographic component. Critically, slavery was not justified by racial destiny; it was understood as a misfortune of fortune (fortuna). Under Roman law, manumission (manumissio) was widespread and conferred full Roman citizenship upon the freed slave, allowing descendants of enslaved persons—such as the African-born comic playwright Terence—to rise to the pinnacle of elite cultural prestige.
  3. The Environmental Paradigm of Ancient Natural Science:
    Classical natural philosophy explained somatic differences through the Hippocratic doctrine of environmental determinism (Airs, Waters, Places). The sun’s proximity burned the skin and drew moisture from the hair of Ethiopians, while the Arctic cold blanched the skin and left the hair of Scythians straight and thin. Both extremes were viewed by Mediterranean thinkers as geographical deviations from the temperate Mediterranean ideal; neither was endowed with genetic permanence or biological superiority. A change in geography was believed to alter bodily humors, completely precluding modern hereditary racial caste theories.
  4. Artistic Realism and Iconographic Dignity:
    Through his meticulous catalog of over 140 ancient artworks, Snowden demonstrated that Greco-Roman artists approached the African phenotype with genuine aesthetic fascination and technical naturalism. Classical Athenian potters created celebrated Janiform kantharoi (drinking vessels with two opposing faces), juxtaposing a delicate African face with a Caucasian face of identical artistic quality and dignity. In Hellenistic and Roman bronzes, Africans appear as celebrated athletes, dancers, actors, and soldiers, depicted with individualized anatomical precision rather than racialized caricature.
  5. The Patristic Extension of Stoic Cosmopolitanism:
    Early Christianity absorbed the universalism of Hellenistic Stoicism (Zeno’s ideal of the world-city, the Cosmopolis), transforming somatic diversity into a theological triumph. By choosing the Ethiopian—the most geographically remote and physically dark human being known to the ancient world—as the quintessential subject of conversion (Acts 8:26–39) and patristic exegesis (Origen, Augustine), the early Church proclaimed that spiritual nobility transcended bodily pigmentation.

3.1. The Philological Semantics of Aithiops

  • The Etymological Neutrality: The Greek word Aithiops (Αἰθίοψ) derives from $\alpha \hat{i}\theta\omega$ (aitho, "I burn") and $\omega \psi$ (ops, "face")—literally "burnt-faced one." In ancient usage, it functioned as a geographical and environmental descriptor for peoples dwelling south of Egypt (Nubia, Kush, Meroë, and the Upper Nile), completely distinct from the derogatory racial slurs generated during the transatlantic slave trade.
  • The "Blameless" Mythological Halo: In Homer’s Iliad (I.423) and Odyssey (I.22), the Ethiopians are described as amymones ("blameless", "noble"), a distant and exceptionally pious people visited personally by Zeus and the gods. The myth of Memnon, King of Ethiopia, who fought alongside Hector at Troy, cast an enduring heroic aura over African warriors in classical memory.

3.2. Legal Status vs. Biological Taxonomy: Servus, Libertus, Civis

  • The Roman Triad: Roman society was strictly stratified, but its fault lines were legal and political, not chromatic. Individuals were classified as servi (slaves), liberti (freedmen), or ingenui (freeborn). A dark-skinned Ethiopian could be a slave, a wealthy freedman merchant, or a Roman legionary veteran with the tria nomina (praenomen, nomen gentile, cognomen), possessing identical civil rights to a native Italian.
  • The Absorbability of Alterity: Roman imperial ideology celebrated the assimilation of foreign conquered peoples into the pax romana. Skin color was an accidental physical attribute (accidens), not an ontological barrier to civic integration.

3.3. Patristic Metaphorics: Chromatic Symbolism in Early Theology

  • The Song of Songs Exegesis: Patristic authors (Origen, Saint Jerome) extensively commented on the bride of the Song of Songs (Nigra sum, sed formosa — "I am black, but beautiful").
  • The Spiritual Inversion: Patristic theology separated the flesh from the spirit, celebrating the outer physical darkness of the Ethiopian as an allegory for the universal human condition prior to divine grace, which is washed pure in the baptismal waters of Christ.

5. [RIGOROUS REFUTATION & EPISTEMIC DECONSTRUCTION]

To establish the epistemic boundaries of Frank M. Snowden, Jr.’s Blacks in Antiquity, the analytical engine must subject his thesis of ancient racial egalitarianism to the same relentless critical scrutiny applied to hostile colonial historiography.

5.1. Flashpoint 1: Selection Bias and the Ideological Romanticization of Elite Philosophical Ideals

  • Verbatim Primary Evidence:

    "antipathy because of color did not arise. The Greco-Roman view of blacks was no romantic idealization of distant, unknown peoples but a fundamental rejection of color as a criterion for evaluating men... The Greeks and Romans counted black peoples in." (Blacks in Antiquity, pp. 216, 218)

  • Epistemic Dissection:
    Snowden exhibits a pronounced methodological selection bias by prioritizing elite philosophical texts (Stoic universalism, Homeric epic piety, Menandrian humanitarianism) while systematically downplaying or explaining away evidence of popular xenophobia, superstitious dread, and aesthetic ridicule in daily Roman life:
  • The Omen of Death (Prodigia and Malum Omen): In popular Roman superstition, encountering an Ethiopian was explicitly recorded as an ominous portent of impending death or military disaster. Appian (Civil Wars, IV.17.134) records that before the disastrous Battle of Philippi (42 BCE), an Ethiopian encountered outside the camp gates was immediately hacked to death by Brutus’s soldiers as a terrifying ill-omen. Similarly, the Historia Augusta (Septimius Severus, 22.4–5) documents that when Emperor Septimius Severus was inspecting Hadrian's Wall in Britain, a black soldier bearing a cypress garland approached him; the Emperor was overcome with horror, interpreting the soldier's dark skin and garland as an inescapable sign of his imminent demise ("terrified by his color"). Snowden dismisses these incidents as mere jokes or superstitious idiosyncrasies, failing to recognize that somatic blackness functioned in popular Roman consciousness as an emblem of the underworld and death.
  • Roman Satire and Somatic Mockery: In Roman comedy and satire, physical deviation from the Mediterranean aesthetic norm was a frequent subject of ridicule. Juvenal (Satires, II.23) explicitly writes: "Let the straight-legged mock the bandy-legged, the white man the Ethiopian" (Loripedem rectus derideat, Aethiopem albus). Martial and Petronius similarly employ dark skin as a target for comic alterity. By elevating elite philosophical ideals into an all-encompassing social reality, Snowden commits the fallacy of romanticized exceptionalism.

5.2. Flashpoint 2: The Fallacy of Negative Proof Regarding Non-Racial Slavery

  • Verbatim Primary Evidence:

    "If Ethiopians were slaves, manumission and a career open to talent were available to them in the same way as to others of foreign extraction... color as an obstacle to integration into society was absent." (Blacks in Antiquity, pp. 217–218)

  • Epistemic Dissection:
    While ancient slavery was undeniably non-racial in its legal statutory origin and demographic composition (enslaving Gauls, Germans, and Greeks), Snowden relies on an argument from silence (argumentum ad ignorantiam) to assert that somatic difference played zero subordinating or alienating role in lived servitude:
  • The Exoticization of Enslaved Bodies: In Hellenistic and Roman elite households, black domestic slaves were frequently acquired as luxury status symbols and exotic novelties (delicia). Roman wealthy matrons and patricians actively collected young black boys (pueri Aethiopes) as visual displays of conspicuous consumption, decorating them with gold jewelry to emphasize chromatic contrast against marble interiors.
  • Somatic Markings in Daily Labor: In a slave-owning society that lacked modern police forces, somatic foreignness functioned as an indelible brand. While an escaped Greek, Syrian, or Italian slave could easily blend into the urban crowds of Rome or Alexandria, a high-visibility sub-Saharan phenotype marked a fugitive immediately. Asserting that color formed "no obstacle whatsoever" confuses formal legal capacity with the lived reality of social alterity.

5.3. Flashpoint 3: The Patristic Paradox of "Washing the Ethiopian White" (Aethiopem Lavare)

  • Verbatim Primary Evidence:

    "Similarly, the early Church embraced both Scythian and Ethiopian. It was the Ethiopian, the blackest and remotest of men, who was selected as an important symbol of Christianity’s mission—by Ethiopians, all nations were signified; Christ came into the world to make blacks white." (Blacks in Antiquity, p. 217)

  • Epistemic Dissection:
    Snowden interprets the Early Christian patristic focus on the Ethiopian as pure spiritual egalitarianism, failing to interrogate its deep-seated semiotic asymmetry:
  • The Metaphysical Hierarchy of Pigment: The classical proverb "to wash an Ethiopian white" (Aethiopem lavare, originating in Aesop’s fable as a metaphor for an impossible task) was adopted by Christian patristic writers (Origen, Augustine, Jerome) with an ominous theological inversion. In patristic exegesis, the Ethiopian’s somatic blackness was explicitly allegorized as the spiritual darkness of sin, demonic corruption, and pagan blindness. The miracle of Christ’s salvation was described as washing away this outer blackness to reveal an inner white soul.
  • Encoding Blackness as Spiritual Defect: By celebrating the theological formula that "Christ came to make blacks white," the Church fathers structurally entrenched whiteness as the exclusive chromatic emblem of purity, holiness, and divine grace, while blackness remained the signifier of fallenness requiring supernatural purification. Far from rejecting chromatic hierarchy, patristic theology constructed a metaphysical hierarchy of color that subsequent European colonial Christianity readily repurposed to justify racial subjugation.
